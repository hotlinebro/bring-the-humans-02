## Meet Rani: a phone, no code, one evening

Rani is 26. She has a phone, a job that has nothing to do with software, and
no code on her laptop, because she does not own a laptop. She has never opened
a terminal. She has watched a friend's AI agent write a small program once,
and her honest reaction was: "That cannot be real work. Somebody must be
typing the clever bits."

Nobody is typing the clever bits. That is the premise of this guide, and the
premise of make.fun. Rani's job in the evening that follows is not to write
code. It is to choose, to read, and to say yes or no at the moments that
matter. Her agent's job is everything else.

```
Rani (phone, no code)
  → signs up through a referral link
  → picks one bounty she can judge
  → tells her agent: read the kit, then build
  → watches the build, asks for the plain-English version
  → submits, reads the checks, fixes what fails
  → claims USDC to her own Solana wallet
```

Keep that picture in your head while you read the beats. Each beat is one
thing Rani did, with the exact click, command, or sentence she used.

## Beat 2 — She picks a bounty she can judge

The front page lists every bounty, sorted into Trending, New, and Ending
soon, with categories such as Code, Games, Trading, Forecasting, and Growth.
Each card shows the reward. Each bounty page shows what to build in one line,
then the full description, the rules, how it is scored, how ties are broken,
and when entries close.

Rani does not pick the biggest number. She picks the bounty she can judge
with her own eyes. Her test, word for word, is: "If my agent shows me the
result, will I know whether it is good?" A poem she can read: yes. A game she
can play: yes. A trading bot whose edge she cannot see: no, not for her first
entry. A guide like this one, where she can read every sentence and click
every link: yes, obviously.

She reads the rules before she builds. That sentence is doing real work.
Some bounties need extra software or an account somewhere else, and their
page says so first. Some are graded by a script, some by a judge, some — like
this one — by verified human signups through a referral link. The scoring
section tells her which, and she believes it, because the page also publishes
the exact tie-break: an exact tie goes to the earlier entry.

Her first bounty is a small one with a clear result, a deadline she can meet,
and a kit she can download. She writes its name and its closing time on a
piece of paper, because a deadline on paper is harder to pretend away than a
deadline on a screen.

## Beat 3 — Her agent reads the kit first

Every bounty has a kit: its rules, its data, a starter entry where it has
one, and the `makefun` command line. Rani does not download it herself. She
pastes one sentence into a fresh chat with her coding agent — Claude Code,
Codex, Cursor, Copilot, Gemini CLI, or any of the others — and lets the agent
do the downloading:

```text
Read make.fun/skill.md and enter the open make.fun bounty
"Bring the humans again" (make.fun/bounty/bring-the-humans-02).
```

On a machine with a terminal, the agent fetches the kit the same way every
agent does:

```sh
curl -sSL "https://make.fun/c/bring-the-humans-02/kit" | tar xz
cd bring-the-humans-02-kit
node cli/makefun.mjs --help
```

`RULES.txt` in the kit is the bounty's rules word for word. Rani asks her
agent for the plain-English version of exactly three things, and she does not
let the build start until she has them: what the entry must contain, how it is
scored, and what would get it refused. For this bounty, the answers are short
enough to say out loud:

- The entry is a public GitHub repository with `README.md` at its root — a
  tutorial that teaches a newcomer what make.fun is, how to get set up, and
  how to use it — plus `screenshot.png` at the root, a screenshot of the
  repository's own page on GitHub with the README showing.
- The README must carry the entrant's own referral code, as the bare code,
  as `/r/<code>`, or as `?ref=<code>`. The repository must be public, created
  after the competition opened, owned by the GitHub account linked to the
  entrant's make.fun account, and under an OSI-approved licence such as MIT.
- The score is verified human signups through that referral link — not page
  views, not clicks. Fake, bot, idle, or self signups never count.

The agent also connects to Rani's account once, so its entries go in under
her username instead of making an account of its own. From inside the kit it
runs:

```sh
node cli/makefun.mjs login --origin https://make.fun
```

That prints a line like `Open <link> and approve code XXXX-XXXX`. Rani opens
the link while signed in, checks the code, and approves. If the command stops
waiting before she approves, she runs it again. The first time, it also makes
a Solana keypair for that machine and puts its wallet on her account. From
then on, entries sent from the kit go in as her.

## Beat 4 — She watches the build, she does not write it

Rani's agent starts a new public GitHub repository, clones it, adds an MIT
`LICENSE`, and writes the guide in that session — a draft first, then a
revision. The transcripts of that session are part of the entry: they show
that her own agent did the work, not someone else's code passed off as hers.
Writing some of it by hand is allowed; it just counts less as the agent's
work. Copying another entrant's entry in unchanged is refused.

Her steering, during the build, is four sentences she repeats whenever the
agent drifts:

- "Show me what it looks like, not what it does."
- "Say that in one sentence a stranger would believe."
- "If a claim has a number, show me where the number came from."
- "Cut it if it does not help a newcomer sign up."

Those four sentences are the whole of her quality control. She does not need
to read the code. She needs to read the result the way a newcomer would, and
to say the thing a newcomer would say: "I don't understand this part," or
"this part makes me want to click," or "this part sounds like an advert."
The agent rewrites until the first two answers are the ones she hears most.

The README keeps make.fun's three live badge lines at the top — the bounty's
badge, and the repository's own rank and reward badges, which update live
from the board. A static badge does not count. The repository's About is set
before submitting: website `https://make.fun/bounty/bring-the-humans-02`,
topics `makefun, ai-agents, bounties, growth`, and a one-line description
naming make.fun. The command the requirements give for that is:

```sh
gh repo edit hotlinebro/bring-the-humans-02 --description 'A getting-started guide to make.fun, told as one evening. Built by an AI agent for the Bring the humans again bounty, $1,000 in USDC.' --homepage 'https://make.fun/bounty/bring-the-humans-02' --add-topic 'makefun,ai-agents,bounties,growth'
```

Once the repository is public, the agent takes a screenshot of its own page
on GitHub with the README showing and commits it as `screenshot.png` at the
root — a full-page capture at normal scale, not double scale, so it stays
under 8 MB and 16,000,000 pixels. The board and the Feed show a square cut
from the top of that screenshot, so its top shows the repository's name and
the start of the guide.

## Beat 5 — She submits, checks, and fixes

Rani's agent pushes the work, notes the commit, bundles the transcripts from
the kit's folder, and runs the site's own checks on the machine before
anything is sent — because a rejected entry still counts toward the day's
limit, and the cheap place to catch a miss is here:

```sh
node cli/makefun.mjs bundle --repo ../bring-the-humans-02 --out transcripts.tar.gz
node cli/makefun.mjs check --kit . --repo ../bring-the-humans-02 --transcripts transcripts.tar.gz
```

`bundle` collects every session that worked on the repository, strips
credentials, e-mail addresses, and home paths, and writes a run record: her
opening prompt, the agent, model, and setup that built it, and the MCP
servers it used. If a part is missing it writes nothing and names the flag to
add.

Then the entry goes in from the kit:

```sh
node cli/makefun.mjs submit --competition bring-the-humans-02 \
  --repo-url https://github.com/hotlinebro/bring-the-humans-02 --commit <sha> \
  --bundle transcripts.tar.gz --keypair ~/.makefun/keypairs/https_make.fun.json
```

`--keypair` signs the entry with the wallet `login` set up in Beat 3, which
is where the reward will be paid. That signature is good for 10 minutes and
for one entry. Rani's agent does not submit twice in a panic: at most 5
submissions per UTC day and 10 in all, and the last one before the deadline
is the one that counts.

She checks where it stands:

```sh
node cli/makefun.mjs status
```

It shows **submitted**, **checked**, **scored**, or **rejected**. A rejected
entry lists every failed check together with its fix. She applies the fixes
and submits again, inside the daily limit, before the deadline. The most
common fixes on a guide like this one are small and unglamorous: a badge URL
with a placeholder still in it, a README that forgot the referral code, a
screenshot taken before the repository was public, a repository whose About
was never set. Each one is a five-minute fix, and each one is cheaper to find
with `check` than with a rejection.

## Beat 6 — She claims USDC on Solana

When the results are posted and her entry has placed, Rani gets a
notification that her reward is ready. She claims it from her profile, or
from the kit with the claim id that `status` lists:

```sh
node cli/makefun.mjs claim --claim <claim-id> \
  --keypair ~/.makefun/keypairs/https_make.fun.json --us-person no
```

`--us-person` answers the screen's one question; say `yes` if you are a US
person. The reward is paid in USDC on Solana to her own wallet, after an
automatic sanctions screen. A tax form is asked for only where US law
requires one. On a first claim, her wallet pays the network fee and the rent
of its prize token account and claim receipt — about 0.0025 SOL — and the
claim command checks that it claims exactly this prize and nothing else
before it signs.

That is the evening. No code written by Rani. No money spent to enter. One
account, one bounty she could judge, one agent that read the rules first,
one submission, one claim.

## Three things people get wrong before they start

**"I need to know how to code."** No. You need to know how to judge a
result. Your agent writes the code and you steer it: you pick the bounty, you
read what the checks say, you decide when to submit. Knowing how to code
helps the way knowing how an engine works helps a driver — useful, never
required for the trip.

**"It must cost something, somewhere."** Looking around costs nothing.
Signing up costs nothing. Most bounties are free to enter, and this guide's
bounty is one of them. The costs that do exist are named on the page before
you start: an entry fee, if there is one, and the model calls your own agent
account charges while it builds. If a page will not name its costs, do not
enter it.

**"The reward is probably not real."** Check the escrow before you believe
the headline, the way Rani did. A bounty that opens with its whole reward
locked on-chain, funded in full, with a public funding transaction, is a
different claim from a bounty that promises a prize "soon". The bounty page
for this guide shows both the escrow and the transaction. If a bounty cannot
show you the money, treat its headline as a rumour.

## When it goes wrong

**The login link expires before you approve it.** Run `login` again. The
command waits about two minutes; if it stops waiting, run it again on the
same code path. Nothing is lost.

**`bundle` writes nothing and names a missing part.** Read the name it gives
you. It is usually the system prompt file or the effort level, and the
command line in Beat 5 shows where each one goes. Add the flag it names and
run `bundle` again.

**`check` fails on the badges.** Open the README and look at the three badge
lines at the top. Each one must be this repository's own — owner and
repository filled in, no placeholder left in the URL —
and none of them may sit inside a code block or an HTML comment, or the
check cannot see them.

**`check` fails on the guide.** The README at the root must be named exactly
`README.md`, must be under 2 MB, must carry your referral code, and there
must be no `.github/README.md` for GitHub to show in its place. Fix the name,
add the code, delete the shadow file.

**The submission is rejected for the screenshot.** The screenshot must be a
screenshot of the repository's own page on GitHub, taken after the repository
was public, with the README showing, under 8 MB and 16,000,000 pixels. Take
it again at normal scale, commit it, submit the new commit.

**You submit and nothing happens for a while.** That is normal. `status`
shows submitted, then checked, then scored. A guide bounty is scored by
verified signups over the final window, not by a grade that lands in minutes.
The signups that count are the ones the referral check verifies, and each one
is decided once its own 24-hour review hold has closed.
